Exercise loops from text: HyperFrames + a mannequin rig

Can we generate our own fitness demo loops (a neutral character doing the exercise, from the catalog description) with HeyGen's HyperFrames? Research run, 3 November 2026. 12 exercises from the real catalog × Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 (high effort).

Verdict: yes, with a framework on top. HyperFrames alone cannot do it: it renders HTML/CSS/JS animation to MP4 deterministically, but has no human motion. Its only "people" are HeyGen avatars (talking presenters, paid credits), not exercise motion. So I built exloop on top of it: a fixed 3D mannequin (Three.js) with forward kinematics plus the physics a model can't do by hand (floor contact, two-point levelling, planted hands and feet via inverse kinematics, balance, seamless loop timing, props, auto camera). The model only writes a small JSON of joint angles over time. With that, every model produced usable loops for 10–12 of the 12 exercises, and HyperFrames renders each one in about 20 s on the Mac Studio, in the app's loop format (1280×720 H.264, 60 fps, no audio, ~110 KB).

Update 7: placements and fluidity measured, framework fixes; final set of 34 loops, none clearly wrong

Every loop is checked at 60 fps over the whole loop: Newton (the floor force never pulls, stays in a friction cone μ 0.8, acts at a zero-moment point inside what touches, peak ≤ 5 body weights, free fall in the air, the hanging pendulum), a rig audit (pops, jerk, sliding, floor penetration, hovering, static balance, grip on the bar) and, new in this update, props (how far the body or a dumbbell cuts into the bench or the bar, from the drawn surfaces) and fluidity (the largest frame-to-frame step of any joint against its neighbouring steps, limit 5×, and the step from the last frame into the first against the loop's own 95th-percentile step).

  • Checks: all 34 loops of the final set pass, every value within 80 % of its limit (worst: Sonnet's lunge balance offset at 80 %). Nothing cuts into the bench or the bar; the worst frame snap is 3.5× (a motion starting), every seam is below the loop's typical step.
  • Framework fixes in this update: the bench edge is placed clear of the body (dips cut 5–11 cm into it); the pull-up bar runs through the palms (it ran through the wrists, hands sticking up); a swinging foot's floor clearance is soft (a hard one let go of the foot with a jump in its speed); the balance offsets are read with a C2 spline (the old cubic's acceleration jumped at each sample, which at 60 fps moved the force point by centimetres); exloop check warns when a planted limb holds the hips below the pose (the cause of the failed lunges: the body sprang up when the foot lifted); SPEC.md got a lunge reference pose and step guidance.
  • Model rounds 10–11: each model fixed its own reviewed issues. Sonnet rewrote its reverse lunge from scratch (it had been rebuilt from Opus's; it is its own work again).
  • Independent review 9/10 (fresh agents, large numbered frames, crops allowed, no metrics): Opus and Sonnet: no loop clearly wrong. Haiku could not author the reverse lunge and the pull-up: after five targeted rounds each (the last with the pose tool and numeric targets) both were still clearly wrong, so they are dropped from the final set (34 loops). Opus and Sonnet do both exercises correctly in the same framework (the checks pass; the poses are wrong: the back leg kicks up, the elbows drive forward). The rest is good or minor (timing, depth, small pose details).
  • Render fix: the jumping-jack videos did not show the motion the checks measured (the layout handed to the render page lost its flight flag in JSON: hips 14 cm too high on landing, knees straight). Fixed; exloop render now refuses to render unless the page draws the checked motion to 1 mm, which all 36 do. Reviewers had rightly called the landings stiff-legged.
  • Jumping jacks, lighter landing: the framework took a long ground phase as one slow sink (hips down 15 cm); now the landing speed is taken in over a short window and the takeoff built over another, the body standing between (knee bend on landing 45–56° instead of 62–68°, all checks pass). A new front_three_quarter camera shows the straddle level and the knee bend. Review 12: all three minor (correct jump, placement and seam; hops a little deep and slow for the cue "light"; the reviewer's "knees caving in" was measured: hip, knee and ankle are in line).
  • New tool: exloop pose <spec> prints per keyframe what a reviewer judges by: foot, knee and hand heights, ankle spread, knee angles, elbows against the shoulders, trunk lean, chin and chest against the bar.

What I learned

  • The framework matters more than the model. All three models wrote sensible angle plans. The failures were mine, in the framework or the spec doc: a wrong hip-hinge example, no balance, kneeling shins not held to the floor, only two support points, a bench too short for knee and hand. Each framework fix lifted all three models at once. Opus and Sonnet's deadlift went from "sitting on the floor" to a correct hinge without touching their specs.
  • Haiku is the value pick. It thinks about 5× longer than Opus but still costs about 1/10, and lands within a few points. It even reasoned around my wrong hinge example (pelvis tilt) that misled Opus and Sonnet.
  • Opus is the most consistent and was best at noticing problems in the spec doc (the bird-dog contact example) and at self-review with renders.
  • Render feedback helps for camera and setup choices (pull-up: all three switched to a readable view and got it right on the second try). It can't fix framework limits; there all three models described the same root cause.
  • Determinism and speed: over 130 renders during the research, 0 failures, about 20 s per loop, 4 in parallel. Loops close seamlessly by construction.

Limits and next steps

  • A mannequin, not a person: no hands with fingers, no face, no muscles. Grips (palms forward or back) are not shown. It reads well on a phone, but it's a diagram, not a coach.
  • No full dynamics: balance, contacts, jumps and steps are solved geometrically and ballistically, not by a physics engine. Momentum moves (kettlebell swings, burpees) and locomotion (inchworm walkout) need more work.
  • Static holds (side plank) need an invented movement or they barely change.
  • To ship: keep the spec doc as a cached system prompt, call Haiku or Sonnet once per catalog entry, run exloop check, render, and let a vision model (or the curator) reject bad loops. Optionally one correction round with the rendered strip.
