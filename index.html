Exercise loops from text: HyperFrames + a mannequin rig

Can we generate our own fitness demo loops (a neutral character doing the exercise, from the catalog description) with HeyGen's HyperFrames? Research run, 3 November 2026. 12 exercises from the real catalog × Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 (high effort).

Verdict: yes, with a framework on top. HyperFrames alone cannot do it: it renders HTML/CSS/JS animation to MP4 deterministically, but has no human motion. Its only "people" are HeyGen avatars (talking presenters, paid credits), not exercise motion. So I built exloop on top of it: a fixed 3D mannequin (Three.js) with forward kinematics plus the physics a model can't do by hand (floor contact, two-point levelling, planted hands and feet via inverse kinematics, balance, seamless loop timing, props, auto camera). The model only writes a small JSON of joint angles over time. With that, every model produced usable loops for 10–12 of the 12 exercises, and HyperFrames renders each one in about 20 s on the Mac Studio, in the app's loop format (1280×720 H.264, 60 fps, no audio, ~110 KB).

Update 2: Newton checks and an independent review (honest status)

The earlier "36 of 36" was measured with my own geometric audit and my own thresholds. This round checks every loop against Newton instead, at 60 fps over the whole loop: the centre of mass's acceleration gives the floor force, which must never pull, must stay inside a friction cone (μ 0.8) and must act at a zero-moment point inside what touches the floor (drawn surfaces within 1 cm, +2 cm); in the air the centre of mass must fall as a projectile.

  • Sliding: at most 4.5 cm per loop for points within 5 mm of the floor; within 15 mm the lifting knee of two bird dogs moves 21–25 cm close to the mat (reads as dragging).

Most are exercise errors in the specs (side plank on the hand instead of the forearm, row without the knee on the bench, bench dips that sit on the bench, low pull-up bar, arms floating in the bridge), some are framework (lunge leaning back, limbs dragging low over the mat in bird dogs). What I learned

  • The framework matters more than the model. All three models wrote sensible angle plans. The failures were mine, in the framework or the spec doc: a wrong hip-hinge example, no balance, kneeling shins not held to the floor, only two support points, a bench too short for knee and hand. Each framework fix lifted all three models at once. Opus and Sonnet's deadlift went from "sitting on the floor" to a correct hinge without touching their specs.
  • Haiku is the value pick. It thinks about 5× longer than Opus but still costs about 1/10, and lands within a few points. It even reasoned around my wrong hinge example (pelvis tilt) that misled Opus and Sonnet.
  • Opus is the most consistent and was best at noticing problems in the spec doc (the bird-dog contact example) and at self-review with renders.
  • Render feedback helps for camera and setup choices (pull-up: all three switched to a readable view and got it right on the second try). It can't fix framework limits; there all three models described the same root cause.
  • Determinism and speed: over 130 renders during the research, 0 failures, about 20 s per loop, 4 in parallel. Loops close seamlessly by construction.

Limits and next steps

  • A mannequin, not a person: no hands with fingers, no face, no muscles. Grips (palms forward or back) are not shown. It reads well on a phone, but it's a diagram, not a coach.
  • No full dynamics: balance, contacts, jumps and steps are solved geometrically and ballistically, not by a physics engine. Momentum moves (kettlebell swings, burpees) and locomotion (inchworm walkout) need more work.
  • Static holds (side plank) need an invented movement or they barely change.
  • To ship: keep the spec doc as a cached system prompt, call Haiku or Sonnet once per catalog entry, run exloop check, render, and let a vision model (or the curator) reject bad loops. Optionally one correction round with the rendered strip.
